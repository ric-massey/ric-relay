The ladder

Everything, to scale

You are about halfway.

Between you and the smallest thing anyone has measured there are eighteen zeroes. Between you and the far edge of what anyone can see, twenty-seven.

Those numbers do not mean anything. That is the problem with them.

So this page does not tell you how big things are. It shows you, at one scale, all the way out — and lets you watch each thing you just looked at shrink to nothing next to the next one.

The arrows at the bottom step you outward one rung at a time, and everything you have already seen shrinks toward the middle of the screen. Stop anywhere to read about what you have landed on, or press Tour and let it walk the whole ladder for you.

How to read it

It is a zoom, not a scroll. The view never moves sideways — the frame stays over the same point and only ever gets wider, so everything grows or shrinks about the middle of the window. Which means the thing you were just looking at does not leave: it collapses toward the centre and turns into a speck while the next thing opens up around it. That collapse is the exhibit.

Rungs fade in as you reach them. Two pairs on this ladder are very close — the Sun is only 1.8 times the width of the Moon's whole orbit — and drawn at full strength the bigger one is a wall of light behind the rung you are actually on. So the next rung up stays dark until you travel toward it, and everything you have already passed stays at full strength.

Things overlap, and that is the geometry. In a zoom that converges on one point, everything smaller ends up inside everything bigger. Some of those are literally true — the Earth is inside the Moon's orbit, the Sun is inside the planets' orbits — and some are just a size comparison sitting on top of itself. The Moon's entire orbit fits inside the Sun with room to spare, and on this page it does exactly that.

The calipers bracket exactly the dimension the caption is quoting — the width of the lakes, the height of the tree, the diameter of a sphere, the gap for a distance. When an object is wider than the window the bracket runs off both sides and its ends become arrows, because a caliper that quietly stops at the edge of the screen is measuring the screen.

The grid is powers of ten, in real metres. As you zoom out the lines slide together, fade, and are replaced by the next decade up, which springs back to full spacing. It is there for the long empty stretches: between the Oort Cloud and the Milky Way there is nothing at all to look at for four and a half decades, and without the grid that reads as the page having frozen instead of as an enormous amount of empty space.

The background belongs to the scale. There is no one star wallpaper following you from the electron to the edge of everything. Below a millimetre the backdrop is suspended matter, because a virus and a blood cell are in water; from the Great Lakes to the Moon's orbit the landscape falls away and stars come up in three layers at different depths; from the nearest star the space between things stops being empty and becomes gas and the band of our own galaxy; by the Local Group there are no stars left in the sky at all, only galaxies; and past Laniakea those resolve into filaments. The star layers converge on the middle of the frame at different rates, which is parallax — pointed along the axis this exhibit actually travels on, since the camera only ever zooms.

Every rung has an idle loop, and each one is a motion the thing really makes. The atom's cloud shifts and does not orbit. The elephant fans an ear, which is what an elephant standing still is mostly doing. The comets in the Oort Cloud are on real Kepler orbits and crawl at aphelion. The Sun throws an eruption every seventeen seconds, at a new bearing each time. Arrive at a rung and it reacts once, then settles into something slow enough to sit and watch.

Why the order looks wrong

The ladder is sorted strictly by size, with no exceptions, and that produces one result people argue with: the largest star anyone has measured is smaller than the planets' orbits. So Stephenson 2-18 lands between the Sun and Neptune's orbit rather than out past the Oort Cloud where a story about "the biggest star" would want to put it.

It is not a bug and it is the most useful thing on the page. A star is a small object in an enormous amount of empty space, and the empty space wins every time you zoom out. The same thing happens at the other end: the Oort Cloud very nearly reaches the nearest star, because a star's territory is not a tidy sphere with a gap after it.

Some rungs are objects and some are distances — the Moon's orbit, the gap to Proxima Centauri. Those say so in the caption, because a distance quietly presented as an object is a lie told by layout.

Where the edges are

The geometry is the data. Every position, every size, every gap and every caliper on the field comes from the metre value in the table below, divided by the width of the current frame. There is no "about this big" anywhere in the layout.

The pictures are illustrations. The sizes are measurements; the drawings inside them are drawn by hand and their colours are chosen. An elephant is grey because elephants are grey, not because anything was sampled. Treat the shapes as labels on correctly-sized boxes.

Nothing is drawn inside a thing that has no measured inside. This rules out three pictures you have seen a hundred times, and all three are wrong the same way — they draw trajectories for things that do not have positions. The electron has no rings, no shells and no axis, because it is a point particle with no known parts. The hydrogen atom has no ball going round it; what shifts there is a probability of being somewhere, which is the only thing an electron in an atom actually has. And the proton has no three coloured dots: it has three quarks, but a quark is confined, has never been seen alone, and cannot be put anywhere in particular. What is drawn instead is the thing that was measured — the proton's charge density, falling off exponentially the way its dipole form factor says it must, with the quoted radius marked as the moment of that curve it really is rather than as the wall it is usually mistaken for.

The rail is not perfectly logarithmic. It is mostly divided by decades — the three empty decades between the electron and the proton genuinely cost you three decades of dragging, and you feel them. But two pairs on this ladder are very close together (you and an elephant; the Oort Cloud and the nearest star), and a strictly logarithmic rail gives those a slice about two pixels wide, which is a rung you cannot stop on. So every gap gets a flat extra 0.45 decades. Across the whole rail that is 9 decades of padding against 45 real ones.

The electron has no measured size. It is drawn at the experimental upper bound with a dashed edge, and the dashes mean we do not know where this stops. Every other rung is a measurement.

Things drawn at true scale that you therefore cannot see. The nucleus inside the hydrogen atom is a hundredth of a pixel. The planets on their orbits are thinner than the lines of the orbits themselves. The Sun disappears entirely at Stephenson 2-18's frame. All three are correct, and all three are the reason the diagrams you have seen elsewhere are wrong.

The Great Lakes are traced from longitude and latitude and projected the way a map is, so the shape you recognize is a shape the numbers produced. It is a coarse trace — about sixty points for five lakes — not a coastline dataset.

Three figures, everywhere. No number here is quoted more precisely than that, because the fourth figure is noise on most of these and completely meaningless on some of them.
