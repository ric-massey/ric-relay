# Crossfire Survey — a world with reasons

Design proposal, 11 September 2026. These are proposed additions, not claims about what is already implemented.

Survey's premise stays: you are a person exploring in the middle of a much larger world. The sector is inhabited near home, stranger and lonelier farther out. Spare vector visuals, direct flight controls, a quiet screen, and rare authored discoveries carry the experience.

The three ideas below belong together. A biome determines what a place provides and what makes travel difficult. Those conditions shape trade and conflict. People live with the consequences and give the player a way to understand them.

## 1. Politics that can be seen from the cockpit

Keep the existing three powers and their identities. Add a small set of needs and decisions, with material consequences.

| Power | What it values | Choices under pressure |
|---|---|---|
| The Cordon | Open routes, reliable supply, its authority over the lanes | Escort convoys, establish checkpoints, clear a blockade, negotiate passage |
| The Hallow Line | Its own roads, territory, autonomy | Warn intruders, close a passage, defend a site, permit limited transit |
| Morrow Compact | Profitable trade and access to resources | Reroute cargo, raise an escort contract, buy passage, back a seizure |

Begin with the existing war pairing. Over time, losses, shortages, incursions, and agreements can change the conflict's intensity. A ceasefire needs a reason; a broken agreement leaves a record. The third faction has interests of its own rather than automatically joining the weaker side.

Leaders can give a faction a particular approach without replacing its identity. A Cordon commander might spend ships to keep a route open; a successor might consolidate around stations. A Morrow representative might prefer paying for access until losses make that policy untenable. Give each recurring leader a name, responsibility, a few priorities, and a memory of relevant outcomes.

The player sees decisions take effect: more escorts, a checkpoint, abandoned freight, a reopened dock. Visual computer bulletins and station data explain the immediate reason. No separate government-management screen is required.

**Example chain:** two water convoys are destroyed → a station's reserves fall → water becomes more expensive → Morrow offers better pay for deliveries → the Cordon sends escorts → pirates shift toward the less protected alternative route. A player can deliver water, escort a hauler, scavenge the wrecks, exploit the prices, or leave.

Keep every stage tied to an actual event. Do not announce a blockade if no route or traffic is affected. Successful deliveries should ease a shortage. Factions need recovery paths so the simulation cannot permanently consume all useful destinations.

### Who the three powers are

The following is proposed lore, extending the existing faction descriptions. Each power should have a defensible purpose, people who benefit from it, and people who pay for it. Flag, occupation, personality, and current orders remain separate: a kind person can serve a coercive institution, and a hostile pilot can be protecting something legitimate.

#### The Cordon — safety that becomes ownership

The Cordon grew from station crews, convoy escorts, and route engineers who kept scattered settlements supplied when travel became unreliable. Its founding argument is simple: a route is only useful if ordinary people survive the journey. Maintaining beacons, rescue craft, and patrols costs something, and somebody has to coordinate it.

Its people value competence, duty, predictable rules, and keeping a promise under pressure. A Cordon maintenance crew repairs a navigation beacon nobody will thank them for. An escort stays with a crippled freighter after the rest of the convoy leaves. These are reasons people willingly support it.

Its contradiction: maintaining a route becomes claiming it. Inspections become compulsory. Temporary checkpoints become permanent. A station that cannot afford protection fees can find itself treated as a security liability. The Cordon can sincerely rescue people and deny them freedom in the same operation.

Internal camps:

- **The Service:** rescue crews, engineers, and commanders who measure legitimacy by safe arrivals. They prefer escorts and repair over punishment.
- **The Directorate:** administrators and security officers who believe fragmented authority creates every recurring crisis. They favor registered cargo, controlled crossings, and unified command.
- **The Frontier Posts:** under-supplied local officers making compromises the center would condemn. Some are practical and generous; some turn their checkpoint into a private toll gate.

Leader concepts: a veteran convoy marshal who remembers the cost of abandoning one ship; a logistics commissioner who will sacrifice a remote station to keep five others supplied; a checkpoint chief whose emergency powers have become their livelihood. Give them trackable decisions rather than moral labels.

Computer-visible identity: precise route diagrams, numbered incidents, escort schedules, inspection seals, and casualty records. Their reports emphasize restored service and protected tonnage. The player can compare those claims with a stranded independent ship beside an immaculate checkpoint.

#### The Hallow Line — belonging that becomes exclusion

The Hallow Line descends from communities that traveled these regions before the modern station network. Its roads connect old habitats, seasonal routes, grave sites, and structures outsiders mistake for abandoned salvage. What looks like unused space on a commercial chart can be somebody's home or history.

Its people value continuity, local knowledge, obligation to particular people, and independence from distant authorities. Pilots know passages a standard chart misses. Communities maintain ships for decades and recover their missing even when the recovery has no commercial value.

Its contradiction: protecting a way of life can mean denying anyone else a place in it. A newcomer who cannot prove a connection may be refused shelter. An old claim can outweigh present need. Protective silence can conceal abuse as easily as it protects a vulnerable settlement.

Internal camps:

- **The Keepers:** custodians of habitats, sites, and routes. They restrict access to preserve places that cannot be replaced.
- **The Open Roads:** traders and younger navigators who want reciprocal access without surrendering sovereignty. They see isolation as another way to disappear.
- **The Return:** displaced families and militant captains seeking sites now occupied by stations or claimed by other powers. Some seek restitution; others will accept only removal.

Leader concepts: a passage custodian who permits humanitarian transit but refuses armed escorts; a navigator trying to open a shared corridor; a displaced commander whose recovered territory contains people born there under another flag.

Computer-visible identity: route marks rather than administrative grids, recurring family or vessel insignia, inherited ship names, and conflicting ownership records. Their boundary notices mark recognized passage and restricted sites. Mystery comes from incomplete knowledge, not from making every Hallow person cryptic or irrational.

#### Morrow Compact — opportunity that becomes dependence

Morrow began as agreements between merchants, dock operators, and freight crews who needed trade to continue across incompatible borders. Shared contracts and pooled defenses made routes possible where neither custom nor public authority could guarantee them. Eventually the merchants' protection fleet became a power in its own right.

Its people value initiative, practical bargains, mobility, and being useful. A good Morrow operator finds a substitute part when everyone else says a station must shut down. Its ports can welcome outsiders whom the other powers distrust, provided they can make a place for themselves.

Its contradiction: everything negotiable includes essentials. Debt can bind a captain to an unsafe route. An apparent rescue can become a salvage claim on the rescued ship. Keeping a shortage profitable can conflict with ending it. Access is open in principle and expensive in practice.

Internal camps:

- **The Route Houses:** established owners protecting reliable long-term trade. They often prefer peace because destroyed customers buy nothing.
- **The Free Berths:** independent captains, dock workers, and small operators who want the Compact to protect opportunity rather than concentrate ownership.
- **The Acquisition Boards:** investors and fleet sponsors who see a disrupted region as an opportunity to purchase ports, seize assets, or secure exclusive supply.

Leader concepts: a port director who keeps neutral docks open during a war; a fleet financier who can make a blockade more profitable than a settlement; a captain elected by indebted crews to challenge an exploitative contract.

Computer-visible identity: cargo manifests, contract seals, ownership transfers, shortage charts, and berth availability. Their reports emphasize restored trade and new investment. A rising cargo throughput graph beside falling independent ship ownership tells a more complicated story.

### Leaders have constituencies, limits, and records

A politician is not a faction-wide personality slider. Give each important leader a position, a constituency, two priorities, a red line, and a history of consequential choices. Their authority has a scope: a port director controls berths and fees, a marshal allocates patrols, and a faction council can authorize wider war.

Material conditions shift influence. Convoy losses strengthen Cordon security hardliners; successful shared passage strengthens Hallow's Open Roads; prolonged trade disruption can undermine a Morrow expansionist. None is an automatic one-event switch. Use sustained pressures, decision intervals, and costs so policy remains understandable.

Leadership changes need a visible cause and method: appointment, council vote, commercial takeover, retirement, or a death confirmed by events. Record the transition in a visual bulletin with a portrait or emblem, office, predecessor, and one concrete policy change. No speeches or conversations.

People within one power can disagree without immediately becoming another faction. Merchant pressure might restrain a Morrow war fleet; rescue officers might win an exemption from a Cordon blockade; a Hallow local authority might allow passage that another community refuses. Show the relevant authority on the computer so these differences do not feel like inconsistent rules.

### Why a war starts, continues, or ends

Wars need an object: a passage, supply source, occupied habitat, disputed station, or enforcement claim. Each side should be able to state what would count as an acceptable outcome internally. The player learns these aims through maps, records, and short attributed bulletins.

Possible conflicts:

- **Cordon / Hallow:** the Cordon opens an escorted supply lane through a Hallow restricted passage. A stranded station needs that route; the passage crosses a protected inhabited site. Alternatives include a negotiated corridor, a longer route, or escalating enforcement.
- **Cordon / Morrow:** a convoy impoundment over disputed cargo and fees becomes a commercial blockade. Both claim to be keeping trade lawful. Shortages pressure nearby stations to choose a side or demand a settlement.
- **Hallow / Morrow:** a Morrow salvage concession includes a structure Hallow communities still claim and maintain. Salvage crews, residents, creditors, and armed protectors have incompatible stakes in the same place.

These are authored scenario seeds, not mandatory histories for every world. Choose an initial dispute consistent with the generated geography and existing war pair. Let actual losses, deliveries, territory, and agreements determine its development. A faction can win a battle while losing the political reason for fighting.

The third power can mediate, trade with both sides, grant passage, or exploit the conflict. Neutrality should be a policy with costs rather than an absence of behavior. Allow limited settlements and enduring grievances; every conflict need not end in conquest or friendship.

### Ordinary people make the politics visible

Retain individual identities for a manageable number of meaningful encounters rather than simulating a biography for every ship. A rescued hauler, a persistent checkpoint officer, a station administrator, and an opposing captain can recur through ship records and changed behavior.

A Cordon freighter and a Cordon patrol share a flag but face different choices. A Morrow captain can dislike the contract they are fulfilling. A Hallow salvager can trade with someone their leaders distrust. Occupation, needs, loyalty, and personal history should influence behavior alongside faction relations.

The player sees a civilian convoy turned away, a neutral dock accepting damaged ships from both sides, or escorts protecting cargo while an unprofitable rescue waits. The computer connects verified observations to the relevant policy. This makes politics legible through what happens to people, without requiring the player to speak or take charge.

### Present it through the computer

Use layered visual records: a faction emblem and territory on the chart; an optional leadership card with a portrait, office, priorities inferred from public decisions, and recent policy history; an incident timeline linking decisions to consequences.

Example: **CORDON EXTENDS INSPECTIONS** above a small map of two new checkpoints. A linked supply chart shows delayed deliveries. A later card, **MEDICAL FREIGHT EXEMPTED**, identifies the responsible office and highlights a reopened route. The surrounding traffic must reflect both changes.

Clearly distinguish a faction's published justification from confirmed facts. The computer can display contradictory claims without deciding that either is true. Never expose private leader motives as knowledge the ship somehow possesses.

Start with one leader per faction and one disagreement inside each faction. Expand named people only when their decisions create visible differences in play. Fen-based learning is optional future work; these identities and pressures can first operate through explicit, testable decision rules.

## 2. Biomes as places to visit and enjoy flying through

**Ric's direction:** biomes need distinctive spaces, silhouettes, visibility, and routes. Some can be completely empty; others can be enormous places you enter. Everything should still look like it belongs in space. Flying itself should be fun and exciting, even before adding rewards, enemies, or news.

The earlier resource-region table below is supporting material. The places and movement experiences in this section now lead the biome design.

| Place | What the player sees | What makes flying there different |
|---|---|---|
| The Empty | A genuinely empty region: no spawned rocks, traffic, salvage, or surprise encounter required to fill it | Uninterrupted travel and room to accelerate; contrast with crowded regions. Show speed and heading through instruments and subtle ship-relative motion cues without adding world objects. Its extent should respect travel time, and entry should be a choice rather than an unavoidable long delay |
| A Cavern Planet | One enormous planetary body with a readable surface, openings, and an interior drawn in cross-section once entered | Wide cave mouths lead to chambers, bends, narrow passages, and alternate exits. Mix precision flying with rooms large enough to build speed. The interior remains the inside of a body floating in space, not an unrelated terrestrial level |
| Asteroid Belts | Long ribbons of rocks, crossings, gaps, and occasional clear lanes rather than an evenly scattered field | Follow the belt, cut across it, weave through gaps, or take a longer clear route. Local density changes the rhythm of thrust and turning |
| Shard Fields | Sharp, angular asteroids with strong silhouettes and varied sizes | Slalom between points and plan turns around clearly visible edges. Collision geometry must match those edges |
| Round Fields | Unusually smooth, perfectly circular asteroids | Broad curved channels and pockets between circles create a different route geometry from shards. Their appearance can be strange without requiring an explanation or implying that their physics are different |
| The Violet Cloud | A massive purple cloud suspended in space, with thin translucent edges and denser interior patches | Fly through shifting visibility: clear windows, obscured routes, and occasional open pockets. Keep near-field obstacles readable soon enough to react at the intended speed; let the scanner complement sight. Do not make unavoidable blind impacts the challenge |
| A Faction Vault | A giant floating box bearing a faction's markings, with entrances and an internal maze | Fly inside an artificial structure: corridors, junctions, large halls, loops, and exits. Access and guarded sections can reflect faction relations. Navigation and architecture should remain interesting even with combat absent |

These are examples to develop, not a requirement to fill every region with every feature. A vast empty reach is allowed to remain empty. A planetary cave or vault can be a specific destination embedded in a broader biome. Belts and clouds can occupy extended regions. Give each a silhouette or approach that makes discovering it memorable.

### Movement comes first

- Build routes around the actual ship's turn radius, momentum, braking, and camera. Tight passages need usable clearance and recovery space; large ships need an alternate route or clear size restrictions before entering.
- Alternate open acceleration, readable turns, and short precision sections. Avoid making every journey either straight-line waiting or constant slow squeezing.
- Offer route choices: a fast exposed crossing, a winding sheltered passage, or a longer clear way around. The choice should be visible enough for the player to make it.
- Place entrances and junctions where the camera can reveal them. A maze needs recognizable chambers, structural landmarks, and a record on the ship's chart so getting oriented is play rather than repeated guesswork.
- Keep all storytelling on the visual computer. Scans can outline an entrance, mark explored passages, or identify ownership; they do not need to reveal the entire interior at once.
- Preserve Crossfire's space identity through silhouettes, scale, surrounding darkness, ship motion, and the continuity of approach and entry. Going inside a place should feel like flying into it.

**Prototype test:** fly a short route through an asteroid belt and a vault or cave using the real controls, with no loot or enemies. If the movement is not enjoyable on its own, revise the geometry before adding content. Compare different hull sizes and phone controls. For the empty region, test whether its contrast and travel time feel deliberate rather than unfinished.

### Supporting ecology and resources

Use broad, persistent regions with recognizable transitions. Keep distance-based danger, but let biome and distance answer different questions: distance tells you how exposed you are; biome tells you what kind of place you have entered.

Each biome should affect at least three existing systems. Color alone is not a biome.

| Biome | Visual identity | Physical and social consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Ice Reach | Pale fractured ice, cold blue outlines, widely spaced light | Water-rich salvage feeds stations; ice fields create tight routes; loaded water haulers attract escorts and raiders |
| Iron Shoals | Dense dark rocks with amber mineral seams | Valuable mining, obstructed lines of fire, slower freight routes; miners and scavengers work around territorial patrols |
| Charged Veil | Thin violet filaments and moving interference bands | Reduced sensor reach and unreliable long-range contacts; pilots navigate by nearby beacons; smugglers value passages patrols struggle to watch |
| Broken Front | Hull ribs, cold engines, occasional active beacons | Salvage and recoverable equipment; unexploded ordnance and unstable wrecks; scavengers, survivors, and contested recovery claims |
| Tidal Corridor | Curved dust bands around paired gravity sources | Predictable slingshot opportunities and hazardous approaches; fast pilots use it as a shortcut; convoys funnel toward safer crossings |
| Outer Quiet | Long darkness, sparse points of light, isolated structures | Few resupply options, long commitments, almost no ordinary traffic; rare signals and authored mysteries become significant |

Give transitions advance cues: increasing ice, changing transponder density, the first distorted scan. Hazards must be learnable and visible enough to plan around. Do not let interference silently invalidate information presented as certain.

Regions persist across saves and visits. A wreck field can record an event within a biome; a major battle should not instantly rewrite the entire region. Keep handcrafted places such as the Leviathan distinctive within their surroundings.

## 3. Stories through the ship's visual computer

The player character does not talk, and nobody talks to the player. All information is presented through the ship's visual computer: scans, incident reports, news bulletins, identification records, and chart updates. No conversations, hails, dialogue choices, speech bubbles, or voiced newsreaders. The computer is an instrument, not a talking companion.

People can still recur as captains, commanders, traders, and faction leaders. Their names, affiliations, portraits or insignia appear in computer records. The player recognizes who survived, returned, changed sides, or disappeared through observation and updated records.

**Make updates visual.** A major event produces a brief card at the edge of the flight screen: an importance label, one-line headline, timestamp, faction emblem, and a tiny map or diagram. Opening it reveals more in the computer; ignoring it leaves flight uninterrupted.

Example bulletin, triggered only by corresponding events in the simulation:

> BREAKING — EASTERN CROSSING CLOSED
> Two Cordon convoys confirmed lost
> Water supply falling at VERRAK
> [Route diagram: two wreck markers, closed segment, destination station]

Other updates show a changing border, escorts gathering, a falling supply gauge, a ceasefire between two faction emblems, a leadership change, or an identified vessel marked SEEN BEFORE with its earlier encounter location. Explanations come from these connected records rather than dialogue.

The computer distinguishes LOCAL SCAN, STATION REPORT, and UNCONFIRMED, with a last-updated time. A wreck confirms a lost ship without necessarily revealing its attacker. Distant events need a reporting source; isolated regions may have old news while local scans still work. Preserve uncertainty and authored mysteries.

Reserve BREAKING for major relevant changes. Routine incidents update existing records. Deduplicate alerts, limit their cadence, and queue lower-priority news for the computer page. No forced pause, full-screen interruption, automatic quest, or mandatory waypoint. A sector timeline, faction diagram, known incident map, and ship history let interested players investigate further.

### Three layers of story

1. **What happened here:** wreckage, cargo, damage, movement, and surviving ships give immediate clues.
2. **Who it happened to and why:** identification records, manifests, news cards, and connected incidents make a remembered history.
3. **What remains unexplained:** authored sites, unusual signals, and incomplete records sustain the larger mystery.

Reveal causes in small pieces. The world provides an immediate cue; a scan or visual bulletin adds context; the computer and chart preserve a concise record. Lead with symbols, diagrams, and short headlines rather than long text logs.

### One possible evening

You leave home and enter the Ice Reach. The computer recognizes a passing hauler from an earlier encounter. Farther out, you find its sister ship broken beside a beacon. Scanning the surviving cargo identifies the destination station.

At that station, the computer refreshes its regional data. A bulletin marks two convoy losses, a water shortage, and an escort response. The route map highlights the disruption; the trading screen shows the increased water price. Outside, Cordon escorts gather. Nobody speaks: the evidence, map, and economy agree. You can help, profit, investigate, or continue exploring.

On a later visit, successful deliveries have eased the shortage—or continued losses have emptied the dock. Computer records preserve the change and any observed involvement. Beyond the freight lane, an unidentified signal leads toward something authored and unfamiliar that the computer cannot yet explain. The world has a history without making the player its appointed hero.

## A small first build

Build one Ice Reach region, two stations, a water route, a trader, a pirate, a patrol, and two recurring named ship records. Connect convoy outcomes to station supply, price, faction response, and three visual computer bulletins. Save the consequences and identities.

Check that a player can understand why the situation changed, find more than one useful response, and return later to recognizable consequences. Also check that leaving the situation alone remains a valid way to play, and that the world can recover from disruption.

Use a lightweight regional simulation for unloaded areas and detailed ship behavior nearby. Both must draw from the same event history so arriving, leaving, or reloading does not duplicate cargo, restore destroyed ships, or contradict a reported outcome. Keep distant simulation bounded to preserve browser and phone performance.

Fen could later supply learned choices within these systems. First establish the actions, observations, and consequences it would learn from. The first playable version needs no neural training, external LLM, or added dialogue service.

**Design test:** does each addition create new situations using what already exists, while keeping flight readable and leaving room for silence and discovery?
