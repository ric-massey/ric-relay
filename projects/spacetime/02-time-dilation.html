Chapter 2 of 4 · Moving clocks run slow

Time Dilation

If everyone measures light at the same speed — and they do — then a moving clock must tick slower than one at rest. Not because it's broken. Because the time between its ticks really is longer. Here's how one rule forces that, and the experiments that caught it happening.

You'll seeparticles from the sky that only reach the ground because their time runs slow.
You'll trythe twin paradox, and a GPS satellite with relativity switched off.

In this chapter

    Part 1

    The light clock

    Build the simplest clock possible: two mirrors facing each other, with a pulse of light bouncing between them. Each trip up and back is one tick. It's a real clock — every clock is just something that repeats — and its rhythm depends only on the speed of light.

    Now take a second, identical clock and send it flying past you sideways. From where you stand, its light doesn't go straight up and down any more. The mirrors move while the light is in flight, so the light has to travel a diagonal — a longer path. And light can't go faster to make up for it: you measure it at the same speed as always. A longer path at the same speed takes longer. The moving clock ticks slower.

    Switch on the triangle. In the time t that one half-tick takes, as you see it, the light goes c·t along the diagonal while the clock moves v·t sideways. The height between the mirrors is the same as for the still clock: c·t₀, where t₀ is the half-tick of a clock at rest. Pythagoras:

    (c·t)² = (c·t₀)² + (v·t)²
    t = t₀ ⁄ √(1 − v²/c²) = γ · t₀ γ ("gamma", the Lorentz factor) is how many of your seconds one tick of the moving clock takes. It's 1 at rest and grows without limit as v approaches c.

    Every clock must agree with the light clock — a wristwatch, a heartbeat, an atom, a cell dividing — or you could stand in a moving ship, compare two clocks and work out your speed, and relativity's first rule (no experiment inside a smooth-moving ship can tell you it's moving) would be broken. So it isn't the clock that slows. It's time along that path.

    Part 2

    How much slower?

    The formula hides a surprise in its shape: for almost every speed, γ is so close to 1 that the effect is invisible, and then near light speed it shoots toward infinity.

    ThingSpeedγIts clock falls behind, per day of yours

    A day of walking costs you less than a trillionth of a second. An astronaut on the space station ages about 28 microseconds a day less from speed alone — measurable, but only with atomic clocks. It takes a particle accelerator to make the effect large.

    Part 3

    Moving through space, or through time

    There's a tidy way to picture the formula. Take your speed through space as a fraction of light, v/c. Take your clock's rate — how many of its seconds pass per second of the person watching — 1/γ. Those two numbers always sit on the same circle:

    (v/c)² + (clock rate)² = 1 Square the clock rate √(1 − v²/c²) and add v²/c²: it's exactly 1, at every speed.
    A picture, not a law. People say "everything moves through spacetime at the speed of light — sitting still, it's all in the time direction." That's this circle in words. It's a good way to remember the result, but spacetime isn't ordinary geometry (part 6 shows where the analogy breaks), so treat it as a memory aid, not the mechanism.

    At light speed the point reaches the bottom of the circle: the clock rate is zero. Light itself doesn't experience the trip at all.

    Part 4

    Proof from the sky: muons

    Cosmic rays hit the top of the atmosphere all the time, about 15 km up, and make muons: heavy cousins of the electron that fall apart after an average of 2.2 millionths of a second. They come down at 99.8% of light speed.

    Do the sum the old way. At nearly light speed, 2.2 µs is enough to travel about 660 metres. To fall 15 km takes 50 µs — over 22 lifetimes. Almost none should reach the ground: about one in ten billion.

    But detectors on the ground count them all day long. Here's why.

    At 99.8% of light speed, γ is about 16. From the ground, the muon's clock runs 16 times slow, so its 2.2 µs stretch to 35 µs — enough that about a quarter survive the fall. This was measured: in 1963, Frisch and Smith counted about 560 muons an hour on top of Mount Washington and about 410 at sea level, 1,900 m lower. Without time dilation they should have counted around 25.

    What the muon says

    Ride along with the muon and its clock is perfectly normal: it does decay after about 2.2 µs of its own time. But from its point of view, the atmosphere is rushing up at 99.8% of light speed, and a moving thing is shorter along its direction of motion by the same factor γ. Its 15 km of air is less than 1 km thick. Both stories give the same count on the ground — time dilation for one observer is length contraction for the other.

    Part 5

    Each sees the other's clock run slow

    Here is the part that sounds like a contradiction. Two ships pass each other. A says B's clock is slow. B — who is entitled to say she is the one at rest — says A's clock is slow. They can't both be right… can they?

    They can, because "B's clock reads 1 when mine reads 2" compares clocks in two different places, and that needs a decision about which events count as happening at the same time. A and B decide differently. Draw it out and the contradiction disappears.

    A's lines of "same time" are flat. Follow the one from B's first tick across to A's worldline: A's clock already reads more than 1. Switch to B's lines — they're tilted, because B is moving (chapter 3 is about exactly this) — and the one from A's first tick meets B's worldline after B's clock has passed 1. Each observer, slicing time their own way, finds the other slow. Nobody is wrong; they're answering slightly different questions.

    Part 6

    The twin paradox

    One twin stays on Earth. The other flies to a star and back. When they meet again they can stand side by side and compare — no "same time over there" needed — and the traveller is younger. But if each sees the other slow, why isn't it symmetric?

    Because the trips aren't symmetric. The Earth twin's worldline is straight. The traveller's has a corner: she turns around, and she can feel it (the engines fire, she is pushed into her seat). And in spacetime, unlike on paper, a straight worldline between two events is the one with the most time on it. Any detour — any corner — has less.

    Try this
    1. Show her yearly signals. Earth receives them slowly for most of the trip, then in a rush at the end. Count them: they add up to her age.
    2. Show Earth's signals. She receives them slowly on the way out and quickly on the way back, and the switch happens right at the turnaround — not at the end, as it does for Earth. That's the asymmetry, made visible.

    Want to take the trip yourself, watching Earth's clock race away out the window? How Speed Affects Time, another exhibit in Exploration, puts you in the pilot's seat.

    Part 7

    Your phone needs this: GPS

    There's a second way to slow a clock, from Einstein's later theory of gravity: clocks deeper in gravity run slower. A clock on the ground ticks a little slower than one up a mountain. In 2010, optical atomic clocks at NIST caught the difference between two clocks just 33 cm apart in height.

    GPS satellites feel both effects at once, in opposite directions. Their speed slows them. Their height — far up out of Earth's gravity well — speeds them up, by more. Move the satellite and see which one wins.

    For GPS the net is about +38 microseconds a day. That sounds like nothing, but GPS finds you by timing radio signals that travel at light speed, and light covers 11.5 km in 38.5 µs. Without the correction, every position would drift by roughly 11.5 km a day. The satellites' clocks are deliberately set to tick slightly slow before launch — 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz — so that up in orbit they keep pace with the ground.

    Check yourself

    Four questions

    You fly past Earth at 0.8 c. What does your own wristwatch do?

    Time dilation is always someone else's clock. In your own frame you're at rest; it's Earth's clocks you'd say run slow.

    Why do so many muons reach the ground?

    At 0.998 c their clocks run about 16 times slow as seen from the ground. From the muon's side, the same fact shows up as the atmosphere being 16 times thinner.

    In the twin paradox, what breaks the symmetry?

    Only the traveller changes frames, and she can feel it. Between two meetings, the straight worldline always has the most time on it.

    GPS satellites' clocks, left alone, would…

    Speed costs them about 7 µs a day; being higher up gains them about 46. The total is a gain of about 38, worth 11.5 km of position.
