Chapter 3 of 4 · Simultaneity

The Loss of Now

Two things happen far apart. Did they happen at the same time? For three hundred years the answer seemed obvious. Einstein showed it depends on how you're moving — and that there is no single "now" stretching across the universe.

You'll learnwhat "at the same time" means for things that are far apart.
You'll seetwo observers disagree about which event came first, and both be right.
You'll trya ladder that fits into a barn shorter than it is.

In this chapter

    Part 1

    Seeing isn't happening

    You can't see two distant events at the moment they happen; you see their light when it arrives. So "these two happened at the same time" is never something you observe directly. It's something you work out: note when each light arrived, subtract how long it was travelling, and compare what's left.

    Einstein turned that into a definition. Two lamps flash. If their light meets exactly halfway between them, they flashed at the same time. Below, two lamps flash together. Move yourself around the room.

    Everyone who does the subtraction honestly agrees, as long as they're all standing still relative to the lamps. The trouble starts when one of them is moving.

    Part 2

    Einstein's train

    A flashbulb goes off in the exact middle of a moving train car. Light runs toward both ends. Someone riding in the car and someone standing on the platform both watch, and both know light travels at the same speed in every direction — for each of them.

    The rider says: the bulb is in the middle, the ends are equally far away, so the light reaches both ends at the same time. The person on the platform says: while the light was travelling, the rear of the car moved toward its pulse and the front moved away from its pulse — so the rear is lit first.

    Each of them followed the same honest rule — light goes at c, subtract its travel time — and they got different answers. Neither made a mistake. Whether two separated events are simultaneous depends on the motion of whoever is asking. (The platform also sees the car a little shorter than the rider does — length contraction, the other face of time dilation from chapter 2.)

    Part 3

    The line of "now" tilts

    On a spacetime diagram, "everything happening now" for someone at rest is a flat, horizontal line. For someone moving, it's tilted — toward the light line, by exactly as much as their own worldline leans the other way. Here is the train again, drawn as worldlines.

    t′ = γ · (t − v·x ⁄ c²) The Lorentz transformation for time. t′ is the time the train's clocks show for an event the platform labels (x, t). Events with the same t′ lie on a line of slope v/c — the train's "now". The v·x term is the whole story: the further away an event is, the more the two frames disagree about when it happened.
    Part 4

    Can first and second swap?

    If simultaneity depends on the observer, can the order of two events flip too? Sometimes. Drag event B and change the speed of the observer whose "now" is drawn.

    Try this
    1. Put B to the side of A, in the elsewhere. Sweep the speed: at one speed B is simultaneous with A, and past it B comes first.
    2. Now put B inside A's light cone. Sweep again. The order never flips, whatever the speed.

    That's the rule, and it's what keeps the universe sane. If one event could have caused the other — if they're inside each other's light cones — every observer agrees on their order. Only events too far apart for any signal to connect can be ordered differently. You can disagree about simultaneity; you can never disagree about cause and effect.

    It's also why nothing can go faster than light. A faster-than-light message is a line in the elsewhere, and some observer would see it arrive before it was sent — they could, in principle, send a reply that arrives before the first message left.

    Part 5

    Walking changes "now" in Andromeda

    The tilt of your "now" is tiny for everyday speeds, but the disagreement it causes grows with distance (that's the v·x in the formula). Make the distance big enough and even a stroll matters. This is known as the Andromeda paradox, after Roger Penrose's telling of it.

    Does that mean walking changes what is "really" happening in Andromeda? No — and this is where chapter 1 comes back. Everything in that span is in your elsewhere. Neither of you can see it, affect it or be affected by it for 2.5 million years. "Now, there" is a label, not something either of you can check. Your "now" is a way of slicing spacetime, and different motions slice it differently.

    Part 6

    The ladder in the barn

    A classic puzzle that only simultaneity can untangle. A ladder twice as long as a barn is carried through it very fast. The barn's owner says: moving things are shorter, so for an instant the whole ladder is inside, and I can shut both doors at once. The ladder's carrier says: from where I'm standing it's the barn that's moving and shorter. There's no way the ladder fits.

    Who's right? Both. Play it in each frame.

    The barn's owner shuts the two doors at the same time — in the barn's frame. Those two door events are far apart, so the ladder, moving, doesn't agree they're simultaneous: for the ladder, the far door shuts and opens again first, while the ladder's front still hasn't reached it, and only then does the near door shut behind the back end. The ladder is never trapped with both doors closed. Everyone agrees on what actually happens — no door ever hits the ladder — and they disagree only about which door moved first.

    Check yourself

    Four questions

    In Einstein's train, who is right about the order the ends were lit?

    Neither frame is special. Each measured honestly; they slice spacetime differently.

    Event A causes event B. Can a fast observer see B happen before A?

    A cause must be inside its effect's past light cone, and order inside a light cone is the same for every observer. Only spacelike pairs can swap.

    You start walking. Your "now" in Andromeda moves by days. Can you use that to learn anything about Andromeda?

    The light from those events won't reach you for millions of years. Moving changes the label "now" on events you can't touch.

    In the ladder-and-barn puzzle, what does the ladder's carrier see?

    The doors closing "at the same time" is simultaneous only in the barn's frame. In the ladder's frame the far door's closing comes first, and it reopens in time.

    Where the ideas come from

    • A. Einstein, "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies" (1905), §1 — the definition of simultaneity by light signals. The train version is from his popular book Relativity: The Special and the General Theory (1916), chapter 9.
    • The Andromeda paradox: R. Penrose, The Emperor's New Mind (1989), after an argument by C. W. Rietdijk and H. Putnam.
    • Distances: Andromeda 2.5 million light-years; the galactic centre about 26,000; the edge of the observable universe 46.5 billion.
