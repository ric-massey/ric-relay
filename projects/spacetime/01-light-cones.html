Chapter 1 of 4 · Causality has a shape

Light Cones

Nothing — no ship, no signal, no cause — travels faster than light. That one rule draws a shape around every moment in the universe: everything that could have caused it, everything it could ever affect, and a huge region it can never touch at all.

You'll learnto read a spacetime diagram — the one picture all four chapters are drawn in.
You'll seewhy "now" on Mars or the Sun is something you can't know yet.
You'll trysix figures you can drag, play and break.

In this chapter

    Part 1

    A map with time going up

    A normal map shows where things are. A spacetime diagram shows where they are and when. Space runs left to right (just one direction of it, to keep things flat). Time runs up the page.

    A single point on it is an event: a place at a moment. "My coffee cup, 9:00 this morning" is an event. A thing that lasts — you, a car, a photon — is a whole line of events, one for each moment. That line is its worldline.

    The trick that makes the diagram useful is the units. Time is measured in seconds and distance in light-seconds — how far light goes in a second, about 300,000 km. In those units, light travels one square across for every square up. Light is always a 45° line.

    Try this
    1. Go from Sitting still through The ISS. Every one of them is drawn straight up. They are not — they're moving — but next to light, even the space station is standing still.
    2. Jump to Half light speed. Only now does the line visibly lean.

    That is the first surprise of the diagram: on the scale that matters to light, everything you have ever seen move is a vertical line. The space station covers 7.7 km every second, and in that second light covers 300,000. Its worldline leans less than a thousandth of a degree.

    Part 2

    Nothing leans past 45°

    The faster something goes, the more its worldline leans. Light's line leans exactly 45°. Nothing with mass can reach that, and nothing at all — not matter, not a message — can lean past it.

    This isn't an engineering limit, like "we don't have a big enough engine." It's built into the geometry: push a ship harder and its speed creeps closer to light without arriving (chapter 2 shows why). Slide past 1 c below and see what a faster-than-light path would mean.

    The two 45° lines leaving an event are the paths of light sent out to the left and to the right. Every possible future for anything at that event lies between them. That wedge has a name.

    Part 3

    The light cone

    Take any event — call it here and now. Light leaving it carves out a wedge going up: the future light cone. Anything inside it, you could reach or send a signal to in time. The same lines extended downward carve out the past light cone: everything that could have reached you, and so could have caused something here.

    Everything else — to the sides, outside both wedges — is elsewhere. Those events are real. They are happening. But no signal can get from them to you or from you to them in time. Physicists call such a pair of events spacelike separated; inside the cone they're timelike, and exactly on its edge, lightlike.

    Try this
    1. Drag the event straight up. It stays in the future however high you go: you can always reach a place you're already standing at.
    2. Drag it 5 light-years to the side and only 2 years up. The dashed line shows light from here arriving 3 years too late.
    3. Put it exactly on the yellow edge. Only light — nothing slower — gets there on time.

    "All that matters is how long the information takes to get to you."

    The elsewhere is not "the future we haven't reached yet" or "the past we've forgotten." It is a third category with no everyday name, because in everyday life it is too thin to notice: on Earth the elsewhere around you right now is only a fraction of a second deep. The next part makes it bigger.

    Part 4

    Every view of the sky is old news

    You only find out about an event when its light — or any other signal — reaches you. Until then it is in your elsewhere: it has happened, and there is nothing anyone where you are could know about it. Pick a place and watch the news travel.

    The yellow wedge under Earth is Earth's past light cone at the moment the clock shows. It is the set of everything Earth can know about. It climbs as time passes, and the event becomes news only when the wedge swallows it.

    • Mars rovers can't be driven with a joystick. A command takes between 3 and 22 minutes to arrive, and the picture of what happened takes as long to come back. Engineers send a day's plan and let the rover drive itself.
    • If the Sun vanished, Earth would keep orbiting the empty spot for about 8 minutes 20 seconds. Gravity's news travels at the speed of light too — the 2017 neutron-star collision GW170817 arrived as gravitational waves and as light within two seconds of each other, after 130 million years on the road.
    • Betelgeuse may already have exploded. It is about 550 light-years away (the measurement is uncertain by a hundred or so). If it went supernova any time in the last five centuries, the news is still on its way.
    Part 5

    Everyone has their own cone

    There is no master light cone. Each event has its own, so two people standing apart have different pasts and different elsewheres. An event can be in your past and in my elsewhere — something you already know and I can't yet.

    Where their cones overlap matters. The overlap of their pasts is shared history: events that could have influenced both of them, such as a common cause. The overlap of their futures is everywhere they could both send a message, or meet.

    Try this
    1. Put the event straight above A, low down. It's in A's future and B's elsewhere: A can affect it, B can't yet.
    2. Find the point where the two futures first overlap. That's the soonest A and B could hear about each other's "now".
    Part 6

    Why it's called a cone

    With one dimension of space, light leaving an event is two dots running apart, and the "cone" is a V. Add a second dimension and the flash spreads as a growing circle, like a ripple on a pond. Stack those circles up the time axis and you get a real cone.

    Our universe has three dimensions of space, so a flash is a growing sphere of light, and the cone is a 4-dimensional shape no one can draw. But every slice you can picture works the same way.

    Check yourself

    Four questions

    On a spacetime diagram, a worldline leans 60° away from straight up. What is it?

    Light leans 45°. Leaning further means covering more space than light in the same time, which would carry it outside its own light cone.

    Tonight you see a star 10 light-years away explode. When did it explode?

    The light took 10 years to reach you. You are seeing the explosion's light arrive, which means it entered your past light cone only now.

    An event is 4 light-years from you and 2 years in your future. Can anything you do now affect it?

    Even light needs 4 years to cover 4 light-years, and it only has 2. Nothing can get there in time.

    The Sun vanishes. How long until Earth stops orbiting it?

    Changes in gravity travel at the speed of light, so Earth keeps swinging around the empty spot until the news arrives — the same 8 minutes 20 seconds the light takes.

    Where the numbers come from

    • Speed of light: 299,792,458 m/s, exact by definition of the metre.
    • Earth–Mars: 54.6 to 401 million km; Earth–Sun 1 AU; Earth–Moon 384,400 km on average.
    • Voyager 1: about 170 AU from the Sun in late 2026 (NASA JPL's mission status page).
    • GW170817: LIGO/Virgo and Fermi, Astrophysical Journal Letters 848 L13 (2017).
    • Betelgeuse: 548 (+90/−49) light-years, Joyce et al. 2020.
