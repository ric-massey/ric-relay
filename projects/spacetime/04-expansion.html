Chapter 4 of 4 · Cosmology

Space Expanding

Every distant galaxy is moving away from us, and the farther it is, the faster it goes. That doesn't put us at the centre of anything. It means the space between galaxies is growing — everywhere at once, with no centre and no edge.

In this chapter

    Part 1

    Everyone is the centre

    Picture galaxies as raisins in a loaf of rising bread. As the dough rises, every raisin moves away from every other one. Raisins that started twice as far apart move apart twice as fast, simply because there's twice as much dough between them to grow.

    Below is a patch of the universe. Press play, then tap any galaxy to stand on it. The arrows show how everything else moves from there.

    Try this
    1. Stand on a galaxy in the middle, then one at the very edge. The picture you see — everything rushing away, faster the farther it is — is the same.
    2. Watch the small graph. Wherever you stand, speed against distance is a straight line. That line is Hubble's law.

    The patch has an edge only because a screen does. The real universe, as far as anyone can tell, is the same in every direction and from every place, and expansion is happening at every point at once. There's no spot it's expanding from. The Big Bang wasn't an explosion at a point in space; it was the moment all of space was much denser.

    Part 2

    Hubble's law, with real galaxies

    In 1929 Edwin Hubble plotted galaxies' distances against how fast they're receding, and got roughly a straight line. Today's measurements give a slope — the Hubble constant — of about 70 km/s for every megaparsec (a megaparsec is 3.26 million light-years).

    Two honest footnotes. First, galaxies also move through space, at a few hundred km/s, because of their neighbours' gravity. Nearby, that motion can beat the expansion: Andromeda is coming toward us at 110 km/s and will merge with the Milky Way in about 4.5 billion years. Second, the slope isn't settled: the early-universe measurement (Planck, 67.4) and the nearby-supernova one (SH0ES, 73.0) disagree by more than either's error bars. This page uses 67.7. Nobody yet knows why they differ — it's called the Hubble tension.

    Follow the line far enough and the speed passes the speed of light, at about 14.4 billion light-years. That doesn't break chapter 1's rule: nothing is moving through space faster than light. The space between is growing that fast.

    Part 3

    Light gets stretched on the way

    Light crossing expanding space is stretched along with it. When it arrives, its wavelength has grown by exactly the factor the universe grew while it was travelling. Astronomers call the stretch the redshift, z:

    1 + z = (size of the universe now) ⁄ (size when the light left) So light with z = 1 left when the universe was half its present size. z = 3: a quarter. The cosmic microwave background, z ≈ 1,100: a thousandth.

    This is how distances to the farthest galaxies are measured: hydrogen glows at fixed wavelengths, so finding its pattern slid toward the red tells you exactly how much the universe grew in the meantime. The most distant galaxies are so stretched that their light, which left as ultraviolet, arrives as infrared — which is why the James Webb Space Telescope is an infrared telescope.

    Part 4

    Why atoms and solar systems don't stretch

    If space is growing everywhere, why aren't you growing? Why isn't Earth drifting away from the Sun?

    Because expansion isn't a force that pulls things apart. It's the momentum of a universe that started out flying apart. Anything held together by a force — gravity, electricity — simply stays together; its parts don't share in the drift. What's left is tiny: the dark energy that makes expansion speed up does push very gently, everywhere. Here's how gently.

    SystemDark energy's push, against what holds it together

    Inside a hydrogen atom, the push is smaller than the electric pull holding it together by about seventy orders of magnitude. For Earth and the Sun, about twenty-two. Even for the Sun's orbit around our galaxy it's a few parts in a million. Atoms, people, planets, solar systems, galaxies and groups of galaxies all hold together. Only the gaps between groups grow.

    Part 5

    A horizon we can never see past

    An old puzzle: an ant walks along a rubber band at a steady pace while someone keeps stretching the band. Does it ever reach the end? If the stretching slows down, yes. If it speeds up, maybe never. Light crossing the universe is that ant — and for about the last six billion years, the stretching has been speeding up.

    A galaxy sends a flash of light toward us today. Choose how far away it is, and watch the light try to get here.

    Try this
    1. Start at 15 billion light-years. That galaxy is receding faster than light, and at first the light actually gets farther away. It arrives anyway.
    2. Push it past the horizon, about 16.6 billion light-years. Now the light never arrives. Nothing that happens there today can ever affect us.
    3. Switch off dark energy. Without acceleration there's no such horizon: given long enough, light from anywhere gets through.

    That boundary is the cosmic event horizon, and it is chapter 1's light cone at the scale of the universe: events beyond it are in our elsewhere forever. As expansion speeds up, galaxies cross it all the time. Over the next hundred billion years or so, everything outside our own gravitationally bound group will slip over the edge and fade into a redshift too extreme to detect. Astronomers then, living in one merged galaxy, might find no sign that other galaxies, or the expansion, ever existed.

    Part 6

    The whole history on one line

    Everything in this chapter comes from one curve: how the size of the universe — the scale factor, 1 today — has changed with time. It's worked out on this page from four measured numbers: the Hubble constant, and how much of the universe is matter, radiation and dark energy. Drag through time.

    Three things to notice. The curve starts steep and bends over: for its first seven and a half billion years, matter's gravity was slowing expansion down. About 7.6 billion years in, the bend reverses: dark energy, whose density doesn't thin out as space grows, took over, and expansion started speeding up. And the light we see from the very beginning — the cosmic microwave background — left from matter that is now about 46 billion light-years away, in a universe only 13.8 billion years old. There's no contradiction: the space it crossed kept growing behind it.

    Check yourself

    Four questions

    Where is the centre of the universe's expansion?

    Uniform expansion looks the same from every point. Every observer sees everything receding from them, so none of them is special.

    Is the distance from Earth to the Sun growing with the expansion of the universe?

    Bound systems don't take part in the expansion. Dark energy's push on Earth is about 10⁻²² of the Sun's pull: nothing measurable.

    A galaxy 15 billion light-years away, receding faster than light, sends us a flash today. Will it ever arrive?

    The light first loses ground, but the Hubble distance grows and it catches up. Only light from beyond about 16.6 billion light-years never makes it.

    Light from a galaxy arrives with redshift z = 3. When it left, the universe was…

    1 + z is the factor the universe grew by: 1 + 3 = 4.
