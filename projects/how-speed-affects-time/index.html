What you're seeing

The sky as it is, from a ship at rest.

At ordinary speeds, the opening sky is a photographic plate: dense, steady, and built to feel like the Milky Way does to a dark-adapted person rather than like a sparse plotting of catalogue points. That same photographic sky surrounds you at every speed: aberration crowds it forward, Doppler shift changes its colour, and beaming changes its brightness. Bright catalogue stars are laid over it at their real positions. Their colour is not a choice: the catalogue gives a colour index, that gives a temperature, and a blackbody at that temperature has exactly one colour. Your motion multiplies that temperature by the Doppler factor, and the same number cubed once more sets how much brighter it gets — beaming. Colour and brightness can never disagree here, because they are the same number.

Movies get this wrong. There is no blue tunnel and there are no streaks: aberration moves stars, it does not smear them. And nothing twinkles — scintillation is something the atmosphere does, not something stars do.

The band across the sky is the Milky Way — not a picture behind the stars, but the light of the hundred billion of them you cannot pick out, going through the same transform. The dark shapes torn out of it are dust: the Great Rift, the Coalsack, the Pipe. It is nearly colourless to the eye, and every photograph you have seen of it in purple and gold got that from a long exposure, not from anyone's eyes.

Wait — doesn't Earth see my clock as slow?

Yes. And Earth is not wrong. While you are coasting, time dilation is completely symmetric — you each see the other running slow, and both of you are right. That is reciprocity, and it is real physics, not an illusion.

The panel above is showing Earth's clock in Earth's frame of reference, which is unambiguous. The comparison only becomes a physical fact when the two clocks are back in the same place — which is what happens when you press Return to Earth. The difference comes from the fact that you changed reference frames and Earth never did.

Scope, and where the edges are

Your clock is not simulated. The traveler's elapsed time is your own device's clock, ticking normally, because that is the entire point: nothing about travelling fast feels like anything. Earth's clock is the calculated one.

Earth's elapsed time is ∫γ dτ along your path, accumulated every frame. That integral is exact for any speed history, including the ramps where the ship speeds up and slows down — so the numbers stay honest even while you are dragging the slider around.

This is special relativity only. Flat spacetime, no gravity, no gravitational time dilation. Accelerations are drawn as short ramps and their g-forces are ignored; the turnaround brings the ship to a full stop before it reverses, which is what makes each leg a plain constant-velocity problem.

The photographic sky stays. The high-resolution opening plate is set into a complete 360-degree panorama of the same sky. Every displayed ray is traced backward through the aberration equation before that panorama is sampled, so motion distorts the photograph itself rather than replacing it. It never twinkles.

The transformed stars. 9,096 of them, from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue (Hoffleit & Warren 1991), J2000, down to about magnitude 8. Real positions, real magnitudes, real colour indices. Positions are frozen at J2000: proper motion and precession are not modelled.

The Milky Way texture is photographic, not a catalogue. It supplies the visual depth of unresolved stars and dust. Its relativistic direction change is exact; its displayed Doppler colour and beaming are compressed into the range a monitor can show.

The opening view is fully dark-adapted. You begin with the sensitivity an observer would have after sitting in darkness, rather than waiting through that adaptation on the page. At this luminance the Milky Way is nearly colourless because rod vision dominates; Doppler colour appears as beaming makes the light bright enough for cone vision.

Colour. Planck's law integrated against the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions, converted to sRGB, gamut-mapped by desaturating toward white rather than clipping a channel. Tone-mapped once at the end. A monitor cannot reproduce a retina's absolute response, so the transformations and relative brightness are physical while the final display exposure is a calibrated representation. Assumes a reasonably dark room and an sRGB display.

The way home. Earth is held straight ahead as a black silhouette against the star field. Its apparent size grows with your distance from home; the Sun is intentionally kept out of this view so you can watch Earth approach.
